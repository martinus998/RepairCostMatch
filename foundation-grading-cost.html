2026 DRAINAGE COST GUIDE

How much does foundation grading cost?

Foundation grading means reshaping soil so surface water moves away from the house instead of collecting against the foundation. It can be a relatively simple landscaping project or a larger earthwork job depending on slope, access, drainage conditions and how much soil needs to move.

The most useful way to compare grading bids is to look at the intended water path. A contractor should be able to explain where runoff currently collects, how the soil will be changed and where the water will go after the work is complete.

What a foundation grading project may include

  • Soil addition: fill can be added near low areas when the grade beside the foundation needs to be raised.
  • Soil removal: high spots may be cut down so water does not become trapped or flow toward the house.
  • Slope correction: the finished grade should direct water away from the structure without creating a new problem elsewhere.
  • Downspout coordination: roof water may need longer extensions, buried piping or another discharge route.
  • Swales or surface drains: larger yards may need shaped channels or drains to carry runoff to a suitable outlet.
  • Imported fill or topsoil: additional material may be required when the existing soil volume or quality is not suitable.
  • Restoration: sod, seed, mulch, beds, edging, pavers or other finishes may need replacement after soil work.

Main factors that change grading cost

The amount of earthwork is only one part of the price. Access can be just as important. An open yard that allows equipment near the work area is very different from a narrow side yard blocked by fences, HVAC equipment, decks or landscaping.

Steep slopes, clay soil, rock, large trees, buried utilities, retaining walls and the need to haul soil in or out can all add labor. If the contractor must protect finished landscaping or rebuild hardscape after grading, restoration may become a significant part of the scope.

Grading is about surface water

Regrading is most useful when runoff from rain or roof drainage is moving toward the foundation or pooling beside the house. It does not automatically solve groundwater pressure, failed perimeter drains, wall leaks or structural cracks.

Why downspouts matter when grading

A yard can have a reasonable slope and still stay wet if roof water is discharged at the foundation. Downspouts can release a large amount of water during heavy rain, so the grading plan should consider where those outlets terminate.

Ask whether the contractor expects existing downspouts to remain, be extended across the surface or connect to a separate underground discharge system. The answer can change whether grading alone is likely to solve the visible problem.

Grading versus a French drain

These repairs solve different parts of a drainage problem. Grading changes the surface slope. A French drain collects water in a gravel-filled trench and carries it through perforated or solid piping toward an outlet.

If runoff is simply moving toward the house because the soil slopes the wrong way, grading may be the more direct solution. If water must be intercepted or routed across a difficult area, a drain may be considered. Some properties use both methods.

Watch the final discharge point

Moving water away from one wall is not enough if the new slope sends it toward another part of the house, a driveway, a low spot or a neighboring property. A useful plan should show where the runoff is expected to go after the grade is changed.

Local drainage rules can also affect acceptable discharge locations. Contractors should account for property boundaries, public drainage systems and any restrictions that apply to the site.

What a clear grading estimate should include

A detailed bid should identify the approximate area being regraded, how much soil will be added or removed, where imported fill will come from, whether excess material must be hauled away and how the final slope will direct runoff.

It should also state whether downspout extensions, swales, drains, utility locating, landscaping removal and restoration are included. Without those details, two grading estimates may not cover the same work.

How to compare two grading bids

Start by comparing the drainage logic rather than the total price. Ask each contractor to explain the existing problem and the proposed water path. Then compare soil volume, equipment access, downspout work, drains, restoration and any disposal or imported-fill charges.

A lower bid may cover only basic soil shaping, while a higher bid may include drainage, material hauling and full landscape restoration. Make sure you are comparing the same scope.

When grading may not be enough

If water appears after every storm despite good surface slope, if the basement leaks through wall cracks, if standing water rises from below or if the foundation wall is moving, the problem may require more than grading.

For basement symptoms after storms, use our water in basement after rain guide. For structural cracks or movement, see the foundation crack guide before choosing a water-control-only repair.

Questions to ask before approving grading work

  • Where is surface water collecting now?
  • Where will the water flow after the project?
  • How much soil is being added or removed?
  • Will imported fill or topsoil be required?
  • Will excess soil be hauled away?
  • Are gutters and downspouts part of the plan?
  • Will any swales, catch basins or drains be added?
  • Are buried utilities or irrigation lines in the work area?
  • Will trees, shrubs, sod or hardscape need to be removed?
  • What landscaping restoration is included?
  • Could the new slope send water toward a neighbor or another part of the property?
  • What happens if poor soil or unexpected drainage conditions are found?

Frequently asked questions

Can foundation grading stop basement water?

It can help when surface runoff is the main source of water near the foundation, but it will not fix every groundwater, wall or drainage-system problem.

Is grading always a large excavation project?

No. Some projects involve modest soil shaping, while others require significant earthwork, imported fill, hauling and landscape restoration.

Should grading be done before waterproofing?

The sequence depends on the cause of the problem. Surface drainage should be evaluated as part of the overall water-control plan so deeper work is not used to compensate for an obvious runoff issue.

Do I need both grading and a French drain?

Not necessarily. The right combination depends on how water moves across the property and where it needs to be collected or discharged.
