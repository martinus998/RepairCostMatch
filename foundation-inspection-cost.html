2026 INSPECTION COST GUIDE

How much does a foundation inspection cost?

Current 2026 consumer cost guides commonly place a standard professional foundation inspection around $300 to $600, while an independent structural-engineer foundation assessment is often around $400 to $700. Larger homes, difficult access, detailed written reports, calculations or repair-design work can push the fee higher.

The important distinction is what you are actually buying. A short contractor visit, a general inspection and a formal engineering assessment are not the same service. Before comparing prices, ask what the inspection includes and what you will receive afterward.

Why foundation inspection prices vary

  • Foundation type: slabs may be faster to review than pier-and-beam, crawl-space or complex basement systems.
  • Home size: larger structures and multiple problem areas take longer to inspect.
  • Access: tight crawl spaces, finished basements or obstructed exterior walls can increase inspection time.
  • Documentation: a verbal evaluation is different from a detailed written or stamped engineering report.
  • Measurements and calculations: level readings, movement measurements, drawings or design work can add scope.
  • Travel and local rates: inspection and engineering costs vary by market.

Contractor inspection versus structural engineer

Some foundation contractors offer free or low-cost evaluations because they also sell repair work. That can be useful when you already know a repair system may be needed and you want a contractor proposal. An independent structural engineer serves a different purpose: the focus is assessment and, when needed, design rather than selling the repair itself.

An independent opinion can be especially useful when a proposed repair is expensive, several contractors recommend different methods, movement appears significant, or you want a written basis before approving piers, anchors, wall reinforcement or major leveling. What a foundation inspection may look at

The exact scope depends on the professional and the home, but a review may include visible crack patterns, floor level changes, wall movement, exterior separation, crawl-space supports, basement walls, drainage clues and other signs that help distinguish isolated cosmetic damage from broader structural movement.

If cracking is the main symptom, start with our foundation crack guide. If you see sticking doors, sloping floors or recurring separation, compare our foundation settling signs guide.

What to ask for in the report

If you are paying for an inspection, ask in advance whether the fee includes a written report. A useful report may document where movement was observed, what measurements were taken, which areas deserve monitoring, whether drainage appears relevant and whether further engineering or repair design is recommended.

For a major repair decision, clarity matters more than a long document. You want to understand what condition was observed, whether it appears active or stable, what additional investigation is needed and what type of repair scope should be compared.

When an inspection can save money

A few hundred dollars spent clarifying the problem can be small compared with a multi-thousand-dollar stabilization project. An inspection may help separate cosmetic cracking, water or drainage problems, isolated support issues and true structural movement before bids are compared.

That does not mean an inspection automatically reduces the repair cost. Its value is in helping you compare the right repair. A low bid for crack sealing is not comparable with a piering proposal if the two contractors are solving different problems.

For budgeting after the diagnosis is clearer, use our foundation repair cost guide. If the issue involves raising or leveling the structure, see our house leveling cost guide.

Questions to ask before booking

  • Who will perform the inspection and what are their qualifications?
  • Is the fee for a site visit only, or does it include a written report?
  • Will measurements, photos or level readings be included?
  • Will the inspector identify likely causes, or only document visible conditions?
  • Is the inspector financially connected to the repair company?
  • Will follow-up questions or a second visit cost extra?
  • If repairs are recommended, will the professional provide drawings or specifications?

When a second opinion can make sense

If one contractor recommends a major stabilization project and another recommends only localized repair, the difference may be worth investigating before you sign. The same is true when a wall, floor or foundation appears to be moving rapidly, or when the repair scope involves many piers, anchors or substantial excavation.
