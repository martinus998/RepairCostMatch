2026 UNDERPINNING COST GUIDE

How much does foundation underpinning cost?

Underpinning is used to strengthen or extend support beneath a foundation when settlement or inadequate bearing conditions require a deeper or more stable load path. Because the work can involve multiple support points, engineering, excavation and difficult access, the total can vary widely.

The most useful way to compare estimates is to understand the proposed system, number of supports, target depth and whether the scope includes lifting, stabilization only or related drainage and restoration work.

What underpinning can include

  • Push piers: driven using the structure as resistance and commonly used for settlement stabilization in suitable conditions.
  • Helical piers: screwed into the ground to a designed resistance and used in a range of foundation and support applications.
  • Concrete or traditional underpinning: may involve excavation and extending the bearing area beneath existing foundation sections.
  • Engineering and monitoring: some projects include structural design, elevations, load calculations or movement monitoring.
  • Lifting or stabilization: a proposal may aim to stop further movement, recover some elevation, or both.

Why pier count matters

The number of support points is one of the largest cost drivers, but it is not the only one. Mobilization, soil depth, equipment access, excavation, engineering and restoration can make the total differ even when two projects use a similar number of piers.

For a narrower breakdown, see our foundation repair cost per pier guide, plus the dedicated helical pier cost and push pier cost pages.

Support locations matter as much as the count

Two proposals can use the same number of supports but place them in different locations. Ask each contractor to mark the proposed pier positions and explain which foundation sections those supports are intended to stabilize.

If one bid uses a much different layout, compare the movement measurements, structural assumptions and repair goal before comparing price.

Access and soil conditions can change the scope

Limited side-yard access, interior installation points, slabs, porches, landscaping and utilities can make work more complex. Required depth can also vary with soil conditions and the bearing layer the repair design is trying to reach.

Interior points may require slab cutting or restoration. Exterior points may require excavation and landscaping repair. Ask which of those items are included.

Stabilization and lifting are different goals

A stabilization project is designed to reduce or stop additional movement. A lifting project attempts some recovery of lost elevation. Those scopes are not automatically interchangeable and may involve different risks, labor and expectations.

Ask the contractor to state the goal in writing. If lifting is proposed, ask what movement is realistically expected and how the structure will be monitored during the process.

Engineering, permits and documentation can change the bid

Some projects include structural engineering, elevation surveys, load calculations, permits or post-installation documentation, while others list those items separately. Compare the professional-services scope before assuming one contractor is simply more expensive.

If an independent assessment is part of the project, compare our structural engineer foundation cost guide.

Drainage can overlap with settlement

Water, erosion, plumbing leaks or poor surface drainage can affect soil conditions around the foundation. Underpinning can address support without necessarily correcting the water source.

If drainage is part of the problem, review foundation drainage repair cost, foundation grading cost and foundation waterproofing cost.

Restoration can be a hidden difference between bids

Concrete patches, landscaping, patios, interior flooring or other finishes may need restoration after installation. A lower structural quote can become a higher total project cost if restoration is excluded.

Ask whether concrete repair, backfill, compaction, cleanup and landscaping are included or left to the homeowner.

Compare the design, not just the total

One bid may include more piers, engineering, lifting, plumbing checks, permits or concrete restoration while another excludes them. Ask each contractor to identify support locations on a plan and explain why that layout fits the observed movement.

Questions to ask before signing

  • What evidence shows that underpinning is needed?
  • What type of pier or underpinning system is proposed?
  • How many support points are included and why?
  • Where will each support be installed?
  • Is the goal stabilization, lifting or both?
  • How is installation depth or resistance verified?
  • Are engineering, permits and inspections included?
  • Are concrete repair, backfill and landscaping restoration included?
  • Does drainage or soil moisture need separate correction?
  • What warranty applies and what does it cover?

Watch for settlement symptoms, but do not self-diagnose

Stair-step cracks, separated trim, sticking doors and sloping floors can appear with settlement, but those signs are not proof that underpinning is required. Start with our foundation settling signs guide and seek an appropriate on-site evaluation for significant or progressing movement.

Frequently asked questions

Is underpinning the same as installing piers?

Piers are common underpinning systems, but underpinning is a broader term for strengthening or extending foundation support.

Is stabilization the same as lifting?

No. Stabilization aims to stop movement; lifting attempts to recover elevation and may be a more involved scope.

Why do contractors recommend different pier counts?

They may use different measurements, support spacing, soil assumptions or repair goals. Ask each contractor to explain the design basis.

Should drainage be reviewed too?

Yes when water or erosion may be contributing to changing soil conditions around the foundation.
