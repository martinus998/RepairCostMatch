How much does crawl space wood rot repair cost?

Wood rot in a crawl space is not one standardized repair. A contractor may be replacing a short damaged joist section, sistering several joists, rebuilding a sill plate, repairing a rim joist, replacing a beam or adding temporary support while larger structural members are changed.

The most useful way to compare bids is by the damaged component, repair method and moisture source rather than one broad average. A localized repair can be far smaller than a project involving multiple beams, joists and load-bearing supports.

Common structural wood scopes

  • Floor joists: individual members may be sistered, reinforced or replaced. See our floor joist repair cost guide.
  • Crawl-space beams: larger load-bearing members can require temporary support and staged replacement. Compare crawl space beam repair cost.
  • Sill plates: damage where framing meets the foundation can require careful load transfer. See sill plate repair cost.
  • Rim joists: perimeter damage may interact with siding, insulation and exterior moisture. See rim joist repair cost.
  • Posts and supports: damaged or inadequate support points can add another structural scope beyond the wood itself.

Why the damaged component matters

A short noncritical section of decay is different from damage at a beam bearing point, sill plate or multiple joists. Repairs that affect the load path may require temporary support, more demolition and a more careful sequence.

Ask the contractor to identify the exact members on the proposal instead of using a broad phrase such as “repair crawl-space framing.” That makes competing bids much easier to compare.

Sistering versus replacement

Sistering adds new structural material alongside an existing member when the remaining framing and connection details make that approach appropriate. Full replacement may be recommended when decay is extensive, connections are compromised or there is not enough sound material to support a reinforcement detail.

For joist-specific work, compare the joist sistering cost guide. Ask what length of new material is included, how it is fastened and whether damaged bearing areas also need repair.

Temporary support can be a major part of the project

Load-bearing beams, joists and sill areas may need temporary shoring while damaged material is removed. Tight crawl-space access and low working height can make that process slower and more labor intensive.

Ask whether temporary posts, beams or jacking are included in the quote and whether any floor leveling is part of the structural scope or priced separately.

Fix the moisture source separately

Replacing damaged wood without addressing chronic moisture can leave new material exposed to the same conditions. Drainage, standing water, humidity, plumbing leaks, vapor control or encapsulation may need a separate plan.

Use our crawl space moisture repair cost guide, crawl space drainage cost and encapsulation cost guide to separate prevention work from structural repair.

Mold and rot are related but not the same scope

Moisture can lead to both biological growth and structural deterioration, but cleaning a surface does not restore lost structural capacity. If mold remediation is proposed, compare the crawl space mold remediation cost guide separately.

A detailed estimate should state which framing is being repaired and which surfaces are being cleaned or treated.

What can make the repair more expensive

Tight access, extensive decay, temporary shoring, plumbing or electrical lines through the framing, finished floors above, pest damage, multiple connected members and difficult material removal can all increase labor. Structural work may also require permits or engineering depending on the project and location.

Hidden decay can also expand the scope after finishes or insulation are removed. Ask how change orders will be handled if additional damage is discovered.

What a detailed estimate should show

  • Which exact joists, beams, sill plates or rim-joist sections are damaged.
  • Whether each member will be sistered, reinforced or replaced.
  • Temporary support or shoring.
  • Fasteners, connectors and bearing repairs.
  • Plumbing or electrical relocations.
  • Insulation or finish removal and replacement.
  • Moisture-control work included or excluded.
  • Cleanup, debris removal and warranty.

Questions to ask before approving the bid

  • Which exact joists, beams, sill or rim sections are damaged?
  • Will members be sistered, reinforced or fully replaced?
  • How much sound material remains?
  • Is temporary support included?
  • Are plumbing or electrical relocations included?
  • What moisture source caused the deterioration?
  • Is moisture control priced separately from structural repair?
  • What happens if hidden damage is discovered?
  • Is engineering or permitting needed?

How to compare two wood-rot proposals

Compare the exact members, repair length, replacement versus reinforcement method, temporary support, utilities, cleanup, moisture-control work and warranty. A lower bid may simply cover fewer damaged components.

Frequently asked questions

Can encapsulation fix rotten wood?

No. Encapsulation can help control moisture, but structurally damaged wood still needs separate repair.

Can rotten joists always be sistered?

No. The condition of the remaining wood, bearing points and repair design determine whether sistering is appropriate.

Is mold remediation enough?

No. Cleaning does not restore structural strength that has been lost to decay.

Should moisture control be on a separate line?

Yes when possible, because it helps distinguish repair of existing damage from prevention of future moisture exposure.
