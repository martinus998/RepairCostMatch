How much does joist sistering cost?

Joist sistering reinforces an existing floor joist by fastening new structural material alongside it. It can be a relatively focused repair when the existing framing is still suitable for reinforcement, but the final price changes quickly when access, moisture damage, floor jacking or several joists are involved.

Current 2026 consumer cost guidance from Angi places simple sistering at roughly $100 to $300 per joist on the low end. That figure should not be treated as a whole-project quote. A repair involving several joists, finished-floor removal, plumbing, crawl-space access, structural leveling or broader rot can move well beyond that range.

What sistering actually does

The new member is installed alongside the existing joist so the two work together. The repair may be used to add stiffness, reinforce localized damage or help restore capacity after a joist has been compromised. The exact fastening pattern, bearing length and material choice depend on the structure and local requirements.

Sistering is not the same as replacing a joist. Replacement removes damaged structural material, while sistering relies on the existing framing remaining suitable enough to be part of the repaired assembly.

What changes the cost

  • Number of joists: a single accessible joist is a different project from a room-wide repair.
  • Access: tight crawl spaces, ducts, pipes and wiring can slow the work.
  • Joist length and span: longer members and difficult bearing points increase labor and material needs.
  • Floor jacking: if the floor must be raised or supported before reinforcement, temporary shoring and careful lifting add scope.
  • Moisture or rot: damaged framing may require removal and the moisture source may need repair first.
  • Subfloor and finishes: access from above can require removing flooring, subfloor or trim.
  • Engineering and permits: structural alterations may require professional design or local approval.

When sistering may make sense

Sistering can be considered when the existing joist has localized damage or inadequate stiffness but still provides an appropriate base for reinforcement. A contractor or engineer may also use sistering when correcting a bouncy floor or strengthening framing around a modified area.

If the floor is sagging and the cause is still unknown, start with our sagging floor repair cost guide. A low or bouncy floor can come from joists, but it can also involve a beam, post, pier or foundation condition.

When replacement or a larger repair may be needed

Severe rot, termite damage, failed bearing points, major notching or drilling, split framing or a larger support-system problem can make a simple sistering scope inappropriate. In those cases, the contractor may propose partial or full joist replacement, beam work, post replacement or foundation support.

Use our floor joist repair cost guide to compare sistering with replacement. For crawl-space framing, also see crawl-space beam repair cost, crawl-space support post cost and crawl-space wood rot repair cost.

Questions to ask before approving a bid

  • Why is sistering appropriate for this joist instead of replacement?
  • How many joists are included in the scope?
  • Will the new joist run full length or only through the damaged area?
  • Does the floor need temporary support or jacking first?
  • Are plumbing, wiring or HVAC obstructions included in the labor?
  • Is the moisture, rot or support problem that caused the damage also being corrected?
  • Are permits, engineering, subfloor repair and finish restoration included?
