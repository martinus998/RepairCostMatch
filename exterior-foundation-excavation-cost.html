2026 EXCAVATION COST GUIDE

How much does exterior foundation excavation cost?

Excavating beside an existing foundation is usually more complicated than ordinary yard digging because the contractor is working next to a finished structure, often at greater depth and with limited equipment access. Current 2026 cost guides commonly place digging around an existing house or basement at about $5,000 to $15,000, while smaller or simpler excavation work can be less.

That range is only a planning reference. A short, open-access section in easy soil is not comparable with a deep basement wall behind landscaping, utilities, a deck or a narrow side yard. The best way to compare quotes is to separate excavation from the drainage, waterproofing or structural repair that comes after the wall is exposed.

What drives exterior foundation excavation cost?

  • Excavation depth: exposing a basement wall can require substantially deeper work than a shallow drainage trench.
  • Linear feet exposed: opening 10 feet of wall for a localized repair is very different from excavating an entire side or full perimeter.
  • Access: fences, neighboring buildings, decks, patios, retaining walls and narrow side yards can limit equipment size and increase hand labor.
  • Soil and rock: dense clay, saturated soil, boulders, hardpan or rock can slow digging and create extra removal cost.
  • Utilities: gas, electric, water, sewer and communication lines can affect where equipment can work and may require coordination before excavation begins.
  • Soil handling: excavated material may stay on-site, be hauled away or be replaced with more suitable backfill.
  • Restoration: sod, planting beds, pavers, concrete, asphalt, fencing and final grading can add meaningful cost after the trench is closed.

2026 cost benchmarks

HomeGuide's 2026 excavation guide lists roughly $5,000 to $15,000 to dig around an existing house and also reports about $100 to $250 per linear foot for access around an existing foundation. Its broader residential excavation range is lower because many projects are shallower and easier to access.

Angi's September 2026 excavation data gives a broader national residential range of about $1,658 to $6,707, with an average near $3,970, while noting that basement-related excavation commonly reaches $5,000 to $15,000. These figures are useful for orientation, but an existing-foundation project can rise well beyond ordinary yard excavation when access, depth, rock or restoration are difficult.

Excavation is usually only one part of the project

Homeowners often see one large total for “foundation waterproofing” or “foundation repair,” but that quote may combine several distinct scopes. The digging itself creates access. The repair work happens after the wall is exposed.

A larger project may include excavation, wall cleaning, crack repair, waterproof membrane, drainage board, French drain or footing drain work, stone, sump or discharge work, backfill, compaction and final landscape restoration. Ask for those pieces to be shown separately whenever possible.

If the main goal is water control, compare our foundation waterproofing cost guide and foundation drainage repair cost guide. If the proposal includes trench-and-pipe drainage, see foundation French drain cost.

Excavation for waterproofing versus structural repair

The same trench can be used for very different jobs. Exterior waterproofing may require enough wall exposure to clean, repair and coat the foundation. Structural work may require access to cracks, footings, wall sections or other components. Those scopes should not be treated as interchangeable just because both require digging.

If a contractor is recommending excavation because of settlement, large cracks or wall movement, compare the excavation portion with the actual repair scope in our foundation repair cost guide. For major movement or an unclear cause, an independent evaluation may help before committing to a large excavation project; see structural engineer foundation cost.

Backfill and restoration can change the final bill

The project is not finished when the repair is complete. The trench still has to be closed and the property restored. Ask what material will be used for backfill, whether excess soil leaves the property, how final grading will be handled and which surfaces are included in restoration.

Landscaping is one of the easiest items to overlook. Mature plants, irrigation, sod, pavers, sidewalks and driveway edges can add cost even when the excavation itself is straightforward. If final surface drainage is part of the plan, compare our foundation grading cost guide.

Why limited access matters so much

Equipment efficiency changes quickly when the contractor cannot bring a standard excavator close to the wall. Smaller machines may fit but move less soil per hour. In very restricted areas, more of the work may need to be staged or completed with additional labor. Access can also affect where excavated soil is temporarily stored.

When comparing bids, ask the contractor to explain what machine access they are assuming. Two estimates can differ simply because one contractor expects open equipment access while another has included restricted-access labor and haul-away.

Common estimate exclusions to check

  • Rock or boulder removal beyond normal soil excavation
  • Utility relocation or repair
  • Permit, survey or engineering fees
  • Soil testing or disposal of unsuitable material
  • Imported gravel, stone or select backfill
  • Waterproofing membrane or drainage board
  • Drain pipe, sump equipment or discharge work
  • Concrete, paver, driveway or sidewalk restoration
  • Landscape replacement and final grading

Questions to ask before approving excavation

  • How many linear feet of foundation will be exposed?
  • What approximate excavation depth and working width are included?
  • What access limitations are already included in the price?
  • Is soil stored on-site or hauled away?
  • What happens if rock or unsuitable soil is encountered?
  • What backfill material is included?
  • Are drainage stone and pipe separate line items?
  • Who is responsible for utility locating and permits?
  • Does the quote include final grading?
  • Which landscaping, pavers, concrete or asphalt surfaces will be restored?
  • Is waterproofing or structural repair priced separately from excavation?

How to compare two excavation bids

Put both estimates side by side and compare the same categories: linear feet, depth, access assumptions, soil removal, disposal, backfill, drainage material, permits, restoration and the actual repair that follows excavation. A lower excavation number can become the more expensive project if major items are excluded.

Also check whether the contractor has priced only the first visible condition or has included contingencies for common discoveries such as rock, damaged drainage or additional wall repairs. The goal is not to choose the longest estimate; it is to understand what each total actually covers.

Excavation beside an existing structure can involve trench collapse, underground utilities and structural hazards. This page is for cost planning and bid comparison only, not excavation instructions.
