Vertical foundation crack: what to check before repair

A vertical or near-vertical crack in a poured-concrete foundation can have several explanations. Some are associated with shrinkage as concrete cures, while others may reflect movement, settlement or a weak point that now allows water into the basement. The direction of the crack is only one clue.

The useful question is whether the crack is stable, changing, displaced, leaking or appearing with other signs of movement. That context is what makes contractor recommendations easier to compare.

Start with five observations

  • Width: note whether the crack is hairline, clearly open or wider in one area than another.
  • Displacement: check whether one side sits forward, backward, higher or lower than the other.
  • Location: cracks near openings, corners, beam pockets or known settlement areas may deserve additional context.
  • Change over time: dated photos help distinguish a stable crack from one that is progressing.
  • Water entry: repeated leakage can justify repair even when structural movement is limited.

Shrinkage and movement are different questions

Concrete can crack as it cures and shrinks. A stable narrow vertical crack may therefore have a different explanation from a crack that is widening or displaced. A visual pattern alone cannot prove the cause, which is why a clear contractor proposal should describe what evidence supports the diagnosis.

If several movement symptoms appear together, compare our foundation settling signs guide. For a broader overview of crack patterns, see foundation cracks.

Water can make a small crack a bigger practical problem

When soil outside the wall becomes saturated, water can use a crack as an entry path. In that situation, a contractor may recommend injection or sealing, but the surrounding drainage conditions still matter.

Look at roof runoff, downspouts, grading and drainage around the house. If water is repeatedly collecting near the wall, compare foundation grading cost, foundation drainage repair cost and foundation waterproofing cost.

Injection, sealing and structural stabilization are not the same job

Some stable poured-concrete cracks may be candidates for a localized injection or sealing repair. That scope is different from stabilizing a foundation that is still moving.

Ask the contractor whether the goal is water sealing, bonding the concrete, cosmetic repair, structural stabilization or more than one of those. For price planning, compare our foundation crack repair cost guide and basement wall crack injection cost.

How to document the crack before comparing bids

Take a clear photo with a ruler or another consistent reference and record the date. Repeat the photo from roughly the same position. Note rain-related leakage, seasonal changes and nearby symptoms such as doors that begin sticking or floors that feel different.

A cosmetic patch that later separates is useful information because it can show that the repair surface changed. It does not by itself prove why the movement occurred.

When an independent opinion can help

If the proposed repair is large, expensive or structural, an independent assessment can help separate diagnosis from sales. A foundation inspection or structural engineer foundation evaluation may be useful when contractors recommend very different solutions.

What a clear repair estimate should include

  • The contractor's explanation of the crack and whether it appears active or stable.
  • The repair goal: sealing, bonding, stabilization, water control or a combination.
  • The repair material or system and why it was selected.
  • Surface preparation and any finished-wall removal.
  • Drainage or waterproofing work if moisture contributes to the problem.
  • Interior or exterior restoration that is included or excluded.
  • Warranty terms and what happens if leakage or movement returns.

Questions to ask before approving repair

  • What evidence suggests this crack is stable or active?
  • Is there measurable displacement?
  • Is the main problem water entry, movement or both?
  • Why is this repair method appropriate for this crack?
  • Could drainage be contributing to repeated leakage?
  • Would an independent inspection change the scope before a major repair?

Frequently asked questions

Does every vertical foundation crack need repair?

No. The need for repair depends on movement, leakage, displacement, location and the homeowner's goals.

Can a stable crack still leak?

Yes. A crack may be structurally stable and still allow water through when exterior soil is saturated.

Should I patch a crack before monitoring it?

If you are trying to observe change, document the crack clearly first so later comparisons are useful.

Is a vertical crack less serious than a horizontal crack?

Crack direction is only one factor. Width, displacement, movement and the surrounding wall condition matter too.
